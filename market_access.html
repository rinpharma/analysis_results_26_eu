ARS and ARDs in Action: Market Access

Same Recipe, New Kitchen, New Diners

ARS: the recipe populations, subsets, groupings, methods, operations as data

ARD: the meal the result records an executed recipe yields

LinkML: the template what a valid recipe looks like

The CSR kitchen cooks for one diner: the regulator

One base ingredient, the trial data (SDTM, ADaM, CSR). One menu, written by the sponsor in the SAP. One meal, the CSR tables.

Market access serves a table of HTA assessors

Each with their own tastes: a comparator, a population, a template. Same base ingredient, plus ingredients the sponsor does not grow: published aggregates from a literature review, real-world data, registries, expert elicitation. The recipe has to adapt, not just re-run.

In the first published Joint Clinical Assessment, the HTA assessors could not reproduce most of the naive safety comparisons. The error was in an ingredient: event rates extracted from the comparator’s publication had been switched between two outcomes. It was caught because an HTA assessor tried to recompute the numbers from the dossier and could not. [1]

[1] Joint Clinical Assessment Report, Tovorafenib, v1.0, endorsed by the HTA Coordination Group 30 Apr 2026, published 9 Jun 2026, Appendix D.2, request 4 (p. 138): “The assessors were not able to reproduce the effect estimates shown for the naïve comparisons for most of the safety outcomes”; developer’s response: “the event rates for these two outcomes in the Bouffet 2023 study had been switched around in the analyses”. health.ec.europa.eu/publications/joint-clinical-assessment-report-tovorafenib-ojemda_en

  • Continuity: one strip, no re-definition. Orla’s stack table already reconciles the terminology: “ARD” is not an ARS class; results are OperationResult records inside the reporting event, and “ARD” is the flat dataset the R ecosystem extracts from them. Say “result records” when precision matters.
  • Why ARS exists, in one breath: twenty years ago we standardised what goes in (SDTM, ADaM, define.xml) and the document that comes out; the analysis in between stayed in code and its results in prose. ARS and ARD are the missing middle.
  • The reproduction story: tovorafenib JCA, request 4 of the second request for additional information. Serious AE, discontinuation due to AE and interruption due to AE could not be reproduced; severe AE could. The developer found the Bouffet 2023 event rates for two outcomes switched, corrected them and ran additional quality checks. Note for the discussion: the failure was in an external ingredient, not in the trial data, and it was found by hand.
  • Show of hands, 15 seconds: “Who has worked on an HTA dossier? Keep it up if you wrote code for one.” Calibrates depth, and tells you whom to call on in the discussion.

Two Asks of the Same Base Ingredient

Regulator asks HTA body asks
The question Do benefits outweigh risks for the indication? How much better than what we use today, for whom, and is it worth the price?
Comparator, population The comparator justified at design; the trial population The local standard of care; the population the local pathway treats, often a subpopulation
Ingredients Trial data; external evidence is the exception Trial data re-cut per template, plus literature aggregates, real-world data, expert elicitation, costs and utilities: assembled by the sponsor, often not owned
Output One authorisation, one label One dossier per body: added-benefit category, cost per QALY, negotiated price

Regulatory track: once, EU-wide. HTA track: JCA in parallel with the EMA procedure, then national HTA, pricing and reimbursement country by country (EU mean 597 days from authorisation to availability, EFPIA W.A.I.T. 2025). Reg. (EU) 2021/2282, Recital 4, Art. 1(2), 2(6); Eichler 2010.

  • Purpose of the slide: enough context for the discussion, not an HTA course. The two tracks read the same base ingredient and ask different questions; the HTA question forces new analyses and new ingredients.
  • Regulatory: benefit-risk on quality, safety and efficacy; the comparator is the one the sponsor justified at design, placebo or active. HTA: relative effectiveness against the comparator(s) that matter in each health system, plus national economic and organisational domains.
  • The Regulation’s Recital 4 names nine HTA domains: four clinical (health problem and current use, technical characteristics, relative safety, relative clinical effectiveness) and five non-clinical (cost and economic evaluation, ethical, organisational, social, legal). The JCA covers the four clinical domains only (Art. 2(6)); the five non-clinical domains and all pricing and reimbursement decisions stay national (Art. 1(2), Recital 14).
  • Timing: the JCA runs in parallel with the centralised procedure (dossier due at the latest 45 days before the envisaged CHMP opinion, Art. 10(1)); the report must be endorsed no later than 30 days after the Commission decision granting the authorisation (Art. 11(1)(a)) and is published after a procedural check; observed lag so far 40 to 69 days. National HTA and pricing follow the authorisation, and that is where most of the delay to access sits: EFPIA W.A.I.T. 2025 (published May 2026, medicines authorised 2021–2024) gives an EU mean of 597 days from authorisation to availability, median 532, ranging from 56 days in Germany to 1,201 in Romania.
  • Value proposition: in Germany the sponsor must demonstrate added benefit (Zusatznutzen) against the appropriate comparator therapy, on the basis of evidence it submits (§ 35a SGB V); in England the economic case is judged against NICE’s cost-per-QALY range, £25,000 to £35,000 since April 2026 (manual PMG36 §6.3). Both are claims the sponsor has to evidence, on top of the regulatory case.
  • Eichler et al. 2010 is the classic statement of the split: regulators license when benefits outweigh risks; payers decide on health benefit relative to existing treatment options.

The EU Joint Clinical Assessment in One Picture

How it runs [1]

  • Member States’ PICOs are consolidated into one scope with several PICOs; one JCA dossier is filed in parallel with the EMA application
  • A published report on relative effectiveness and safety, no value judgement, no price; national bodies “give due consideration”, then decide benefit, price and reimbursement
  • Commission tracker, extract of 10 Sep 2026: 23 started since January 2025, 4 published, 16 running, 3 discontinued (two for failing the Article 9 dossier requirements); published reports carried 1, 7, 8 and 12 PICOs [2]

Key clinical evidence in the dossier [3]

  • Per PICO: efficacy, safety, all subgroups with interaction tests, AEs by SOC and PT; each analysis flagged as prespecified or not, multiplicity-controlled or not, and who requested it
  • Where no head-to-head trial answers a PICO: indirect comparisons (Bucher, NMA, MAIC, STC) with their input data, model choices and convergence checks
  • CSRs, protocols and analysis plans as appendices; no individual patient data; code only “if the analyses and corresponding calculations cannot be described by a specific standard method”

[1] Regulation (EU) 2021/2282, Art. 7(2), 8, 9(1), 11, 13. [2] European Commission, JCA tracker (ongoing, completed, discontinued), data extracted 10 Sep 2026; published JCA reports: lurbinectedin (1 PICO), tarlatamab (7), tovorafenib (8), onasemnogene abeparvovec (12 in the final scope). [3] HTACG, guidance on the JCA dossier template for medicinal products (adopted 28 Nov 2024), App. A.2, A.3, B.2, D.3, D.5; guidance on multiplicity, subgroup and sensitivity analyses (10 Jun 2024).

  • JCA is joint but not binding: it replaces the clinical part of national assessments in spirit, not the national decision. “No value judgement” is Article 9(1).
  • The dossier template asks for interaction p-values for all subgroup analyses, full results where p < 0.05 or where a Member State asked, and all subgroup results in Appendix A.3. Adverse events by SOC/PT at ≥5% incidence in Appendix A.2, serious and severe regardless of incidence. The multiplicity guidance requires, for each analysis, whether it was prespecified, whether multiplicity was controlled, and who deemed an unplanned analysis necessary (sponsor, regulator, HTA body).
  • Indirect comparisons: dossier section 4.3.4 and Appendix D.5 (evidence-synthesis reports with protocols and SAPs); the reporting checklist in Appendix B.2 asks for software and version, code for non-standard methods, input data with derivation, MCMC chains, burn-in, iterations and convergence, the digitisation method for Kaplan-Meier curves, model and covariate selection, overlap and effective sample size, and results with and without population adjustment.
  • Appendix B.2: “the HTD is not required to provide individual patient data, only aggregated data such as effect estimates with standard errors are required”. Patient listings are supplied only on an Article 11(2) request and are not published. Appendix D.3 asks for code and output only for non-standard methods. So: documents and, conditionally, code. No datasets.
  • Tracker, 10 Sep 2026: 16 ongoing, 4 completed, 3 discontinued; catequentinib and the TIL therapy Tacquell were discontinued on 22 Jun 2026 because the dossier “fails to meet the requirements laid down in Article 9(2), (3) and (4)”; sasanlimab because the marketing application was withdrawn. Completed: tovorafenib (8 PICOs, only 1 assessable), tarlatamab (7), lurbinectedin (1), onasemnogene abeparvovec IT (12 PICOs across 6 populations in the final scope of 26 Feb 2026; several without comparative data).
  • Three of the four completed assessments rested on indirect comparisons: unanchored MAIC with an effective sample size of 5.82 (tovorafenib), Bayesian NMA plus MAIC on digitised curves (tarlatamab), anchored and unanchored MAIC plus ML-NMR (onasemnogene).
  • Article 13(1)(d): Member States may not re-request at national level what was submitted at EU level. HTACG Guiding Principles on Data Transparency (adopted 14 May 2026): trial results and evidence-synthesis methods are not commercially confidential; the dossier and its appendices are published, with redactions.

What HTA Analytics Look Like in Practice

Volume

  • German benefit dossiers grew from about 750 to 3,500 pages on average after the 2020 template change; outliers reach 20,000 [1]
  • Mandatory subgroups (sex, age, severity, region, stratification factors) for every endpoint [2]
  • Safety by SOC and PT with RR, CI and p, repeated per subgroup: one safety annex ran to 926 pages [3]
  • IQWiG sets aside 33% of AE analyses and 73% of subgroup categories submitted [4]

Ingredients that are not trial data: a meal rebuilt from its photograph

  • Comparators the trial never had: literature aggregates, external controls from real-world data and registries, expert elicitation where data are absent [5]
  • Survival curves digitised from a PDF and turned back into patients who never existed, then fed to NMA or population adjustment [6]
  • Three of the first four published JCAs rest on indirect comparisons; one on an effective sample size below six [7]
  • Template rules decide what exists: 10 patients and 10 events per subgroup, interaction p below 0.05, incidence above 5% [2,8]

Outputs that are not tables

  • Extrapolation parameters, transition probabilities and utilities feed a cost-effectiveness model; NICE requires it fully executable, and its HTA assessors re-analyse it in 93 of 100 appraisals [9,10]
  • Countries file on different data cuts. Nobody requires an index of everything produced; the company needs one to know the totality of its own evidence
  • A dossier is read by HTA assessors who never see the patient data

  • The German numbers are the strongest case for “structure at scale”: the dossier grew 4 to 5 times after 2020, mostly AE and subgroup analyses, and IQWiG disregards a third of AE analyses and nearly three quarters of subgroup categories. Around €800k to €1m and 12 months per dossier (vfa 2022; Schweitzer 2024).
  • Macabeo et al. (PharmacoEconomics Open 2024;8(1)): 22% of 543 oncology HTA reports across five countries used an indirect comparison; 30% of those were accepted and about half were left unclear. The JCA experience so far is harsher: tovorafenib’s MAIC matched on an effective sample size of 5.82.

The photograph line is the one metaphor extension worth making: pseudo-IPD reconstruction really is recovering a dish from a picture of it.
  • Right column: the model is the consumer of many results, and the HTA assessor re-analyses the model while never seeing patient data. The data-cut point is about the company’s own sanity: a filing in one country may rest on interim analysis 2, in another on the final cut; efficient production and an accurate index are for the sponsor to know what it has produced, not because anyone mandates it.

each HTA body

= thousands of result records, and every one is a PICO plus an analysis: population, intervention, comparator, outcome. ARS names the same ideas differently: analysis set, grouping, data subset, method. Same recipe structure, different vocabulary: aligning it, and extending it where HTA has words ARS does not, is the next two slides.

  • Message: the volume is not exotic analyses, it is ordinary analyses multiplied by factors the sponsor does not control. That is exactly the situation a specification-driven approach is meant for.
  • Populations and comparators are coupled: a subpopulation defined by the local pathway usually implies a different comparator, so a new PICO. Subgroups within a PICO do not create new PICOs (HTACG scoping guidance, Nov 2024).
  • Model inputs are analyses too: parametric survival fits for extrapolation, transition probabilities, utilities from mapping PRO instruments to EQ-5D with country value sets. They are produced from the same ADaM and rarely appear in a CSR.
  • The waste argument cuts both ways: IQWiG sets aside 73% of subgroup categories (Schweitzer 2024). Own it: automation makes the waste cheaper, not smaller. Navigability is the way out.
  • Ask the room: how much of your HTA programming is genuinely new analysis, versus the same analysis with a different population, comparator or threshold?

What ARS Has No Word For Yet

Criteria for execution

“PT only if incidence ≥ 5%.” “Subgroup curve only if the interaction is significant.” The rule tests a result, not a record, so no where-clause can express it. The template decides what exists.

Record the rule, or only its outcome?

Ingredient provenance

A digitised curve, an event rate copied from a publication, an expert’s prior. ARS can name a source document; it cannot say how the ingredient was made. The first JCA’s error lived exactly here.

Record the recipe for the ingredient too?

Where a number goes

ARS knows a result’s origin and its display. A survival parameter that feeds a cost-effectiveness model has a destination, not a table cell.

Record where a number goes, or only where it came from?

Environment and change

One field for the language and version. Version numbers that say which, never what changed. A delta dossier is a diff nobody can compute.

Record the environment nobody re-runs? Record the diff?

Record as much as possible? How much is too much, and for whom? Each field on this slide costs a statistician time now; the benefit lands later, and often with someone else. That trade is the discussion.

  • Position of the slide: these four are the concepts ARS v1.0 has no class for, as opposed to the previous slide’s mechanisms that exist but lack vocabulary. Tooling for some of them exists outside ARS (the hta-pharma packages encode AMNOG criteria; survival packages hand parameters to models), which is why this is a discussion, not a gap list: nothing here is integrated into the standard.
  • Criteria: the G-BA and JCA rules (10 patients and 10 events, interaction p < 0.05, incidence ≥ 5%) all test results, not records.
  • Provenance: the tarlatamab MAIC code reads a file literally called pseudo_ipd.csv. Nothing in the dossier’s metadata says how that file was made. The tovorafenib error was a switched event rate copied from a publication.
  • Environment: this row feeds Prompt 3 in the discussion. The version question feeds the delta-dossier reality: a JCA report may not exist when the German dossier is due (EFSPI 2025).
  • Hand over to the “who asks” slide: before deciding what to record, look at who is asking for anything at all.

Where This Leaves Us

Re-use is highest in HTA

One recipe, many diners, many cuts. A machine-readable recipe pays back on every re-cook, and nowhere is it re-cooked more.

A shared standard is what makes shared tooling possible

Thousands of records to navigate, trace and diff. Sponsors need that for QC, HTA assessors to find what they asked for. Nobody builds a viewer for an in-house format.

Nobody has asked, and every field has a cost

No regulator or HTA assessor names ARS. Each field costs a statistician time now; the benefit lands later, and often elsewhere: a later dossier, a partner, an HTA assessor.

What would we want to achieve, and what would it cost to capture? Reproducibility, efficiency to reproduce, or legibility for a reviewer and for a machine: pick the goal before picking the metadata. Over to the discussion.

  • Card 1 answers “why would HTA benefit more”: not because HTA analyses are harder, but because the same analysis is re-specified more times, by more parties, on more deadlines. Re-use is where a specification pays back.
  • Card 2: a standard is not only about reproducing a number, it is what lets a common navigator exist. Sponsors already need one internally to QC thousands of outputs; HTA assessors need one to find the analysis they asked for. Keep it as a question if challenged: would an HTA assessor use a viewer the sponsor built, and should they?
  • Card 3 pre-empts the expert’s question (“why a standard and not a good internal format?”) and names who pays: the cost falls on the sponsor’s statistician; the benefit on a future team, a partner or an HTA assessor. That externality is why voluntary standards stall.
